What is a Pituitary Tumor?

A pituitary tumour is an abnormal growth that develops within the pituitary gland. Most pituitary tumours are known as pituitary adenomas and are non-cancerous. However, because the pituitary gland controls the body's hormonal system, even a benign tumour can affect multiple organs and body functions.

Pituitary tumours may produce excessive amounts of hormones, resulting in hormonal disorders, or they may grow large enough to compress nearby structures, particularly the optic nerves, leading to visual disturbances.

The condition is highly treatable, especially when diagnosed early. Treatment may involve observation, medication, surgery, radiation therapy, or a combination of these approaches depending on the individual patient's condition.

Types of Pituitary Tumors

Understanding the type of tumour is important because treatment varies accordingly.

01

Functioning Pituitary Tumours

These tumours produce excess hormones, leading to specific hormonal disorders.

Examples include:

  • Prolactin-secreting tumours (Prolactinomas)
  • Growth hormone-producing tumours (Acromegaly)
  • ACTH-producing tumours (Cushing's disease)
  • TSH-producing tumours
02

Non-Functioning Pituitary Tumours

These tumours do not produce excess hormones but may grow large enough to compress surrounding structures, causing headaches, vision changes, or reduced pituitary function.

03

Microadenoma

Microadenomas measure less than 10 mm in size and are often detected during evaluation for hormonal abnormalities.

04

Macroadenoma

Macroadenomas are larger than 10 mm and are more likely to cause pressure symptoms such as visual disturbances and headaches.

What Does the Pituitary Gland Do?

The pituitary gland is often called the "master gland" because it controls the function of several other endocrine glands throughout the body.

It helps regulate:

  • Growth and development
  • Thyroid function
  • Adrenal gland function
  • Reproductive hormones
  • Fertility
  • Menstrual cycle
  • Milk production
  • Water balance

When a pituitary tumour affects hormone production, patients may experience a wide variety of symptoms that extend beyond the brain itself.

Causes and Risk Factors

The exact cause of most pituitary tumours remains unknown. However, certain factors may increase the likelihood of developing the condition.

These include:

  • Genetic conditions such as Multiple Endocrine Neoplasia Type 1 (MEN1)
  • Family history in rare cases
  • Hormonal and cellular changes
  • Increasing age

Most pituitary tumours occur without any identifiable cause and are not related to lifestyle factors.

Symptoms of a Pituitary Tumor

Symptoms vary depending on whether the tumour produces hormones or compresses nearby structures.

Common symptoms include:

  • Persistent headaches
  • Blurred or double vision
  • Loss of peripheral vision
  • Hormonal imbalance
  • Fatigue
  • Unexplained weight gain or weight loss
  • Irregular menstrual cycles
  • Infertility
  • Reduced sexual function
  • Excessive milk production
  • Enlargement of hands or feet
  • Changes in facial appearance

Large tumours may also compress the normal pituitary gland, leading to reduced hormone production and additional endocrine problems.

When Should You Consult a Pituitary Tumor Specialist?

Consult a pituitary tumor specialist if you experience:

  • Persistent headaches with vision changes
  • Hormonal abnormalities
  • Irregular menstrual cycles
  • Unexplained infertility
  • Excessive milk discharge
  • Symptoms of acromegaly
  • Cushing's disease symptoms
  • MRI showing a pituitary tumour

Early evaluation helps determine whether observation, medication, or pituitary tumor surgery is the most appropriate treatment.

Diagnosis of Pituitary Tumor

Accurate diagnosis requires both neurological and endocrine evaluation.

01

Clinical Assessment

The consultation includes a detailed medical history, neurological examination, and review of hormonal symptoms.

02

Hormonal Blood Tests

Blood investigations evaluate hormone levels produced by the pituitary gland, helping identify functioning tumours and endocrine abnormalities.

03

MRI Pituitary

MRI is the preferred imaging study for diagnosing pituitary tumours. It provides detailed information about tumour size, location, extension, and its relationship to the optic nerves and surrounding structures.

04

Vision Assessment

Because pituitary tumours often compress the optic chiasm, visual field testing is commonly recommended to evaluate the extent of vision involvement.

Personalized Treatment Planning

Treatment decisions are based on:

  • Tumour size
  • Hormonal activity
  • Vision changes
  • MRI findings
  • Overall health
  • Patient's symptoms
  • Risk of tumour progression

Treatment Options for Pituitary Tumor

Treatment depends on the tumour type, hormonal activity, and its effect on surrounding structures.

Management may include:

  • Radiation therapy
  • Long-term endocrine follow-up

For many patients with large tumours, progressive symptoms, or vision changes, surgery remains the most effective treatment.

When is Pituitary Tumor Surgery Recommended?

Not every pituitary tumour requires surgery. Some small, hormone-producing tumours, such as prolactinomas, often respond well to medication and can be managed without an operation. Others may simply need regular monitoring with MRI scans and hormone testing.

However, pituitary tumor surgery becomes the preferred treatment when the tumour is causing significant symptoms, compressing nearby structures, or does not respond to medical therapy.

Surgery may be recommended if:

  • The tumour is causing vision problems or visual field defects.
  • MRI shows a growing pituitary tumour.
  • The tumour is compressing the optic nerves or optic chiasm.
  • Hormone-producing tumours are not adequately controlled with medication.
  • The tumour is causing persistent headaches or neurological symptoms.
  • There is pituitary apoplexy (sudden bleeding into the tumour).
  • A tissue diagnosis is required.

The decision is always individualized after reviewing MRI findings, hormone levels, visual assessment, and the patient's overall health.

Pituitary Tumor Surgery

For many patients, pituitary tumor removal surgery offers the best chance of relieving symptoms, preserving vision, restoring hormonal balance, and preventing further tumour growth.

The primary goals of surgery include:

  • Removing the tumour safely.
  • Relieving pressure on the optic nerves.
  • Preserving normal pituitary gland function.
  • Improving hormone-related symptoms.
  • Preventing further tumour enlargement.
  • Obtaining tissue for pathological evaluation when required.

Whenever possible, Dr. Sachin aims for maximum safe tumour removal while protecting surrounding structures such as the pituitary gland, optic nerves, carotid arteries, and nearby brain tissue.

Endoscopic Transsphenoidal Pituitary Surgery

The majority of pituitary tumours can be removed using Endoscopic Transsphenoidal Surgery, which is considered the preferred surgical approach for most pituitary adenomas.

Rather than making an incision on the scalp, the tumour is accessed through the natural nasal passages using a high-definition endoscope. This minimally invasive technique provides excellent visualization of the tumour while avoiding external scars.

Compared with traditional open surgery, endoscopic transsphenoidal surgery offers several advantages:

  • No external incision
  • Better visualization of the surgical field
  • Less disruption of healthy tissues
  • Reduced postoperative discomfort
  • Shorter hospital stay
  • Faster recovery
  • Lower risk of complications in appropriately selected patients

Dr. Sachin's advanced Skull Base Surgery training enables him to perform complex pituitary procedures with precision while preserving critical structures surrounding the tumour.

When is Open Brain Surgery Required?

Although most pituitary tumours are removed through the nose, a small number of complex or very large tumours may require an open surgical approach (craniotomy).

This may be considered when:

  • The tumour extends significantly into the brain.
  • The anatomy prevents safe endoscopic access.
  • There is extensive involvement of surrounding structures.
  • Revision surgery is required in selected cases.

The choice of surgical technique is based entirely on patient safety and the characteristics of the tumour.

Advanced Technologies Used During Surgery

Modern pituitary tumor surgery in Bangalore combines surgical expertise with advanced technology to improve precision and patient safety.

High-Definition Endoscopic Visualization

High-definition endoscopes provide magnified views of the surgical field, allowing excellent visualization of the pituitary gland and surrounding structures.

Neuronavigation

Neuronavigation functions as a GPS system during surgery, helping accurately identify the tumour and navigate safely around critical blood vessels and nerves.

Microsurgical Techniques

Microsurgical instruments allow delicate tumour removal while protecting healthy pituitary tissue, optic nerves, and nearby blood vessels.

Intraoperative Neuromonitoring

Continuous monitoring of neurological function during selected procedures enhances patient safety during complex surgeries.

Skull Base Reconstruction

Following tumour removal, the skull base is carefully reconstructed whenever necessary to reduce the risk of postoperative cerebrospinal fluid leakage and support healing.

Benefits of Pituitary Tumor Surgery

For appropriately selected patients, pituitary tumor surgery provides several important benefits:

  • Relief from pressure on the optic nerves
  • Improvement or stabilization of vision
  • Better control of hormone-related disorders
  • Reduction in headaches
  • Prevention of further tumour growth
  • Preservation of neurological function
  • Improved quality of life
  • Reduced need for long-term medication in selected patients

Early treatment often improves outcomes, particularly when visual symptoms or hormone-related complications are present.

Risks and Possible Complications

Like any neurosurgical procedure, pituitary tumor operation carries certain risks. These vary depending on the tumour's size, location, previous treatment, and overall health of the patient.

Potential complications include:

  • Bleeding
  • Infection
  • Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) leak
  • Temporary or permanent hormonal imbalance
  • Diabetes insipidus
  • Vision changes
  • Injury to surrounding blood vessels
  • Need for hormone replacement therapy
  • Anaesthesia-related complications

Dr. Sachin carefully evaluates each patient before surgery and uses advanced planning, endoscopic visualization, and microsurgical techniques to minimize these risks.

Recovery After Pituitary Tumor Surgery

Recovery following pituitary tumor removal surgery is generally quicker with the endoscopic transsphenoidal approach compared with traditional open surgery.

During Hospital Stay

Patients are closely monitored for neurological function, vision, hormone levels, fluid balance, and signs of CSF leakage. Most patients begin walking and eating within a short period after surgery.

First Two Weeks

Patients are advised to avoid heavy lifting, straining, vigorous exercise, and nose blowing while the surgical area heals.

One Month

Most patients gradually return to work and normal daily activities, depending on their recovery and the nature of their occupation.

Regular follow-up appointments help monitor healing, review pathology results, and assess hormone function.

Hormonal Recovery After Surgery

One of the most important aspects of recovery is monitoring pituitary hormone function.

Following surgery, some patients experience immediate improvement in hormone-related symptoms, while others may require temporary or permanent hormone replacement therapy.

Regular evaluation by the neurosurgical and endocrinology teams helps ensure:

  • Stable hormone levels
  • Proper adrenal function
  • Normal thyroid function
  • Healthy reproductive hormone balance
  • Long-term endocrine health

This multidisciplinary approach supports both neurological recovery and hormonal well-being.

Long-Term Follow-Up

Even after successful pituitary tumor treatment, regular follow-up is essential to monitor recovery and detect any recurrence at an early stage.

Long-term follow-up may include:

  • Periodic MRI scans
  • Hormone blood tests
  • Vision assessment
  • Endocrinology review
  • Neurosurgical consultation

The frequency of follow-up depends on the tumour type, pathology findings, and individual recovery.

Why Early Diagnosis Matters

Pituitary tumours often grow slowly, and symptoms may be overlooked in the early stages. Delayed diagnosis can result in progressive vision loss, hormonal disorders, and compression of surrounding brain structures.

Early diagnosis provides several important benefits:

  • Better preservation of vision
  • Earlier treatment of hormonal imbalances
  • Improved surgical planning
  • Increased likelihood of complete or maximum safe tumour removal
  • Reduced risk of permanent pituitary dysfunction
  • Better long-term quality of life

If you experience persistent headaches, unexplained hormonal changes, or vision problems, seeking early evaluation by a pituitary tumor specialist can help prevent complications and improve treatment outcomes.

What is a pituitary tumour?

A pituitary tumour is an abnormal growth in the pituitary gland. Most are benign (pituitary adenomas), but they can still affect hormone production or compress nearby structures such as the optic nerves.

Does every pituitary tumour require surgery?

No. Some pituitary tumours, particularly prolactinomas, respond well to medication. Small, non-growing tumours without symptoms may only require regular monitoring. Surgery is recommended when the tumour causes vision problems, hormone imbalance, or significant growth.

What is the best treatment for a pituitary tumour?

Treatment depends on the type of tumour, hormone activity, size, and symptoms. Options include observation, medication, surgery, radiation therapy, or a combination of these treatments.

How is pituitary tumour surgery performed?

Most pituitary tumor operations are performed using an endoscopic transsphenoidal approach. The tumour is removed through the nasal passages using a high-definition endoscope, avoiding external incisions.

Is pituitary tumour surgery safe?

Modern endoscopic techniques, neuronavigation, and advanced skull base surgery have significantly improved the safety of pituitary tumour surgery. The overall risk depends on the tumour's size, location, and individual patient factors.

Will my vision improve after pituitary tumour surgery?

If vision loss is caused by pressure on the optic nerves, many patients experience improvement after surgery. The extent of recovery depends on the severity and duration of nerve compression before treatment.

Will I need hormone replacement after surgery?

Some patients may require temporary or permanent hormone replacement if the normal pituitary gland is affected. Regular hormone testing after surgery helps determine whether replacement therapy is necessary.

How long is the recovery after pituitary surgery?

Most patients stay in the hospital for a few days and gradually return to normal activities over the following weeks. Recovery varies depending on the complexity of the surgery and the patient's overall health.

Can pituitary tumours come back?

Although many pituitary tumours are successfully treated, recurrence is possible in some cases. Regular MRI scans and hormone evaluations help detect recurrence early and guide further treatment if required.

When should I seek a second opinion?

A second opinion is recommended if you have been diagnosed with a pituitary tumour, advised to undergo surgery, or would like to better understand your treatment options before making a decision.
