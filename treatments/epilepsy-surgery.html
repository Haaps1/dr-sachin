What Is Epilepsy?

Epilepsy is a chronic neurological condition characterised by a tendency to experience recurrent unprovoked seizures. These seizures occur when groups of brain cells produce abnormal electrical activity, temporarily affecting the way the brain functions.

The symptoms vary depending on the part of the brain involved. Some people remain aware during a seizure but experience unusual sensations, involuntary movements, or sudden changes in their emotions. Others may have episodes of staring, confusion, loss of awareness, or full-body convulsions.

Not every seizure means a person has epilepsy. Seizures may also occur because of certain medical conditions, infections, head injuries, or metabolic disturbances. A specialist evaluates the patient's history and investigation results to determine the likely cause and whether the episodes meet the criteria for epilepsy.

The primary goal of epilepsy treatment is to control seizures, reduce the risk of complications, and help patients maintain their daily activities. Many people achieve good seizure control with appropriate medication and regular follow-up. Patients whose seizures continue despite suitable medication may require further evaluation for advanced treatment options.

Types of Epileptic Seizures

Understanding the type of seizure is essential because it influences the investigations and treatment recommended for each patient.

01

Focal Seizures

Focal seizures begin in a specific area of the brain. Symptoms depend on the affected region and may include unusual sensations, involuntary movements, changes in behaviour, or temporary changes in awareness. Some patients remain conscious throughout the episode, while others may have difficulty responding.

02

Generalised Seizures

Generalised seizures involve networks on both sides of the brain from the beginning of the seizure. They may cause loss of consciousness, muscle stiffening, rhythmic jerking, or brief episodes of reduced awareness, depending on the seizure type.

03

Absence Seizures

Absence seizures are brief episodes during which a person may stare blankly and become temporarily unresponsive. They are more commonly recognised in children and may sometimes be mistaken for daydreaming or inattention.

04

Tonic-Clonic Seizures

Tonic-clonic seizures can involve loss of consciousness, muscle stiffening, and rhythmic jerking movements. Some patients may experience temporary confusion, tiredness, or muscle soreness after the episode.

05

Drug-Resistant Epilepsy

Some patients continue to experience seizures despite appropriate treatment with anti-seizure medications. This may indicate drug-resistant epilepsy and requires further specialist assessment to determine whether the diagnosis, medication plan, or treatment approach needs to be reviewed.

Identifying the seizure type helps guide the selection of medication and determines whether additional investigations or advanced treatments should be considered.

Symptoms of Epilepsy

Epilepsy symptoms vary depending on the type of seizure, the area of the brain involved, and the patient's age. Some episodes are obvious, while others may be subtle and difficult to recognise.

Common symptoms may include:

  • Repeated episodes of staring or unresponsiveness
  • Sudden jerking movements of the arms, legs, or face
  • Muscle stiffening or full-body convulsions
  • Temporary loss of awareness or consciousness
  • Unusual sensations, smells, tastes, or visual experiences
  • Sudden confusion or difficulty responding
  • Repetitive movements such as lip-smacking or hand movements
  • Temporary difficulty speaking or understanding others
  • Confusion, tiredness, or sleepiness after a seizure

A first unexplained seizure should be medically evaluated. Seek emergency medical help if a seizure lasts five minutes or longer, another seizure begins before the person recovers, or the person has difficulty breathing or regaining consciousness.

Early assessment helps identify the possible cause and determine whether further investigations or treatment are needed.

Diagnosis of Epilepsy

Accurate diagnosis is the first step in planning effective epilepsy treatment in Bangalore. The evaluation aims to confirm whether the episodes are seizures, identify the seizure type, and determine whether an underlying brain abnormality or another medical condition may be responsible.

Medical History and Neurological Examination

The consultation begins with a detailed review of the patient's symptoms and medical history. Dr. Sachin G.R. may assess:

  • Description, duration, and frequency of seizure episodes
  • Changes in awareness or consciousness during an episode
  • Previous head injuries, infections, or neurological conditions
  • Family history of epilepsy
  • Current medications and previous treatment response
  • Possible triggers, including sleep deprivation

A neurological examination may assess muscle strength, reflexes, sensation, coordination, balance, speech, and memory. Information from family members or witnesses can also help describe episodes that the patient may not remember.

EEG (Electroencephalogram)

An electroencephalogram (EEG) records the electrical activity of the brain using small electrodes placed on the scalp. It can help identify abnormal electrical patterns and support the diagnosis and classification of epilepsy.

Depending on the patient's symptoms, the doctor may recommend:

  • Routine EEG
  • Sleep-deprived EEG
  • Video EEG monitoring
  • Prolonged EEG monitoring

Video EEG records brain activity alongside video footage of the patient's behaviour. It can help clarify uncertain seizure episodes and is particularly useful when evaluating patients for advanced epilepsy treatment or surgery.

A normal EEG does not always exclude epilepsy. Results must be interpreted alongside the patient's history and other clinical findings.

01

MRI Brain

MRI provides detailed images of the brain and helps identify structural abnormalities that may be associated with seizures.

MRI may help detect:

  • Brain tumours or other lesions
  • Developmental abnormalities
  • Changes caused by previous brain injuries
  • Stroke-related abnormalities
  • Areas of scarring
  • Certain vascular abnormalities

MRI findings can also help determine whether a patient with drug-resistant epilepsy may benefit from further evaluation for epilepsy surgery.

02

CT Scan

A CT scan may be recommended when an urgent assessment is required or when MRI is not immediately available or suitable. It can help identify certain acute abnormalities, including bleeding or other structural changes.

The choice between CT and MRI depends on the clinical situation and the information required.

03

Additional Investigations

Some patients may require further investigations depending on their symptoms and the suspected cause of seizures.

These may include:

  • Blood tests to identify relevant metabolic or medical abnormalities
  • Neuropsychological assessment
  • Functional brain imaging in selected cases
  • Additional investigations as part of pre-surgical epilepsy evaluation

The results help guide diagnosis, treatment selection, and long-term management.

Common Causes and Risk Factors

Epilepsy can develop for several reasons. In some patients, a specific cause is identified, while in others, the cause remains unknown despite detailed evaluation.

Possible causes include:

  • Previous head injury
  • Brain tumours or other structural brain lesions
  • Stroke or previous brain damage
  • Brain infections such as meningitis or encephalitis
  • Congenital brain abnormalities
  • Genetic conditions associated with epilepsy
  • Lack of oxygen around the time of birth
  • Certain developmental or neurological disorders

Factors that may increase the likelihood of epilepsy include a family history of the condition, severe head trauma, previous brain injury, and certain neurological diseases.

It is important to distinguish between the underlying causes of epilepsy and factors that may trigger an individual seizure. Sleep deprivation, missed medication doses, and other personal triggers may increase seizure risk in some patients, but they do not explain every case of epilepsy.

Identifying the possible cause helps determine the appropriate treatment and whether additional investigations or advanced procedures should be considered.

Treatment Options for Epilepsy

The goal of epilepsy treatment is to achieve the best possible seizure control while preserving the patient's safety, independence, and quality of life. The treatment approach depends on the type of epilepsy, the cause of seizures, their frequency, the patient's age, overall health, and response to previous treatment.

Many patients achieve good seizure control with medication. Others may require a review of their diagnosis or medication plan, while selected patients with drug-resistant epilepsy may benefit from surgical evaluation or neurostimulation.

Treatment options may include:

  • Anti-seizure medications
  • Lifestyle measures and seizure-trigger management
  • Regular neurological assessment and follow-up
  • Epilepsy surgery for carefully selected patients
  • Vagus nerve stimulation (VNS) in appropriate cases
  • Specialist treatment for children with epilepsy

Every patient undergoing epilepsy treatment in Bangalore requires an assessment-based treatment plan. The aim is to select an appropriate option rather than recommend the same treatment for everyone.

Anti-Seizure Medications

Anti-seizure medications are the first-line treatment for most people with epilepsy. These medicines help reduce abnormal electrical activity in the brain and lower the likelihood of further seizures.

The choice of medication depends on several factors, including:

  • Type of seizure and epilepsy syndrome
  • Age and general health
  • Other medical conditions
  • Other medications being taken
  • Potential side effects
  • Pregnancy considerations, where applicable

Some patients achieve seizure control with one medication, while others may require a different medicine or a combination of treatments. Regular follow-up helps the doctor assess seizure frequency, side effects, and response to treatment.

Patients should not stop or change anti-seizure medication without medical advice because sudden changes can increase the risk of recurrent seizures.

Lifestyle Measures That Support Seizure Control

Medication is an important part of epilepsy management, but everyday habits can also influence seizure control in some patients.

Patients may be advised to:

  • Take medication exactly as prescribed.
  • Maintain a regular sleep schedule.
  • Avoid missing medication doses.
  • Identify and manage individual seizure triggers.
  • Follow a balanced diet.
  • Manage stress where possible.
  • Attend scheduled medical appointments.
  • Follow appropriate safety precautions during daily activities.

These measures support medical treatment but do not replace prescribed medication or specialist care.

Drug-Resistant Epilepsy Treatment

Some patients continue to experience seizures despite treatment with appropriate anti-seizure medications. This condition is known as drug-resistant epilepsy, also called medically refractory epilepsy.

Drug-resistant epilepsy is generally considered when two appropriately selected and tolerated medication schedules have failed to achieve sustained seizure control.

Further evaluation may be recommended when:

  • Seizures continue despite appropriate medication.
  • Seizure episodes significantly affect daily life.
  • Medication side effects limit treatment.
  • The seizure type or underlying cause needs reassessment.
  • Advanced treatment options may be appropriate.

A specialist assessment may include a detailed review of previous medications, MRI findings, EEG or Video EEG results, and other investigations. The goal is to determine whether changes to medical treatment, epilepsy surgery, VNS, or another approach may be suitable.

Patients with uncontrolled seizures should discuss further evaluation with a specialist rather than continuing ineffective treatment without review.

Epilepsy Surgery

For carefully selected patients, epilepsy surgery may help reduce seizure frequency and, in some cases, achieve long-term seizure freedom. Surgery is generally considered when seizures remain uncontrolled despite appropriate medication and investigations identify a brain region or abnormality that can be treated safely.

Before surgery is recommended, patients may undergo:

  • Detailed neurological assessment
  • MRI Brain
  • Video EEG monitoring
  • Neuropsychological evaluation
  • Additional functional brain investigations when required

The purpose of this assessment is to identify where seizures begin and understand whether the area can be treated without causing unacceptable harm to important functions such as speech, movement, memory, or vision.

Epilepsy surgery may be considered when a suitable seizure focus is identified and the expected benefits outweigh the risks.

Types of Epilepsy Surgery

The procedure depends on the cause of seizures, the area of the brain involved, and the results of pre-surgical investigations.

Lesionectomy

A lesionectomy involves removing a structural brain lesion that is identified as the source of seizures. It may be considered when the lesion can be treated safely and the findings support a relationship between the lesion and the seizures.

Temporal Lobectomy

A temporal lobectomy involves removing a selected part of the temporal lobe. It may be considered for carefully selected patients with temporal lobe epilepsy when investigations identify a suitable seizure focus.

Corpus Callosotomy

Corpus callosotomy is a palliative procedure that disconnects part of the nerve-fibre pathway between the two cerebral hemispheres. It may be considered for selected patients with severe seizures, particularly those associated with sudden falls and injuries.

Hemisphere Surgery

Hemisphere surgery is reserved for carefully selected patients, often children, with severe neurological conditions and seizures affecting one side of the brain. The decision requires detailed specialist evaluation.

Not every patient with epilepsy is suitable for surgery. Careful assessment is essential to understand the potential benefits, limitations, and risks before making a treatment decision.

Vagus Nerve Stimulation (VNS)

Some patients with drug-resistant epilepsy are not suitable candidates for epilepsy surgery. Others may continue to experience seizures despite medication and previous treatment.

For selected patients, vagus nerve stimulation (VNS) may be considered as an additional treatment option.

VNS involves implanting a small device beneath the skin of the chest. A wire connects the device to the vagus nerve in the neck, allowing it to deliver programmed electrical impulses. The stimulation may help reduce seizure frequency in some patients.

Potential benefits may include:

  • Reduced seizure frequency
  • Shorter seizure episodes in some patients
  • Improved recovery after seizures
  • Better day-to-day functioning
  • Improved quality of life

VNS is not a cure for epilepsy, and the response varies between patients. Benefits may develop gradually as stimulation settings are adjusted during follow-up.

The suitability of VNS depends on the patient's seizure type, medical history, previous treatment response, and overall clinical assessment.

Epilepsy Treatment for Children

Epilepsy can develop during childhood, and the condition may affect learning, behaviour, development, and everyday activities. Early diagnosis helps identify the seizure type and determine the most appropriate treatment for the child's needs.

Epilepsy treatment for children begins with a detailed assessment of the episodes, developmental history, medical background, and relevant investigation results.

Depending on the child's condition, management may include:

  • Anti-seizure medication
  • EEG or Video EEG monitoring
  • MRI Brain when indicated
  • Regular developmental assessment
  • Dietary therapy in selected cases
  • Epilepsy surgery for carefully selected children
  • Vagus nerve stimulation when appropriate

Parents and caregivers should receive guidance on medication adherence, seizure first aid, school safety, and recognising situations that require urgent medical attention.

The treatment plan should be reviewed regularly to monitor seizure control, medication effects, and the child's developmental progress.

Ongoing Follow-up and Long-Term Care

Epilepsy management continues after treatment begins. Regular follow-up helps assess seizure control, review medication effectiveness, identify side effects, and determine whether the treatment plan needs to change.

During follow-up appointments, the doctor may review:

  • Frequency and severity of seizures
  • Response to medication
  • Possible side effects
  • EEG or imaging results when required
  • Sleep patterns and potential seizure triggers
  • Safety at home, school, work, and during daily activities
  • Need for additional investigations or treatment

Patients should maintain an updated list of their medications and inform their doctor about any new symptoms or changes in seizure frequency.

When seizures remain difficult to control, further specialist assessment may help determine whether advanced treatment options should be explored.

Patient Journey for Epilepsy Treatment

The process of epilepsy treatment generally follows several stages. The exact pathway depends on the patient's symptoms, diagnosis, and response to treatment.

  1. 01

    Recognising the Symptoms

    The process may begin after a first seizure or repeated episodes involving staring, involuntary movements, unusual sensations, confusion, or loss of awareness. An unexplained seizure should be medically evaluated.

  2. 02

    Specialist Consultation

    The consultation includes a review of the seizure history, previous illnesses, family history, current medication, and possible triggers. A neurological examination helps assess brain function and identify findings that may require further investigation.

  3. 03

    Diagnostic Evaluation

    The doctor may recommend EEG, Video EEG, MRI Brain, CT scanning when indicated, blood tests, or other investigations. These tests help clarify the diagnosis and identify possible causes.

  4. 04

    Treatment Planning

    The treatment plan is based on the seizure type, underlying cause, frequency of episodes, age, general health, and previous response to medication. Medical treatment is commonly the initial approach.

  5. 05

    Advanced Treatment When Required

    If seizures continue despite appropriate medication, additional evaluation may be needed to determine whether epilepsy surgery, VNS, or another treatment option is appropriate.

  6. 06

    Monitoring and Recovery

    Follow-up appointments help assess seizure control, medication response, side effects, and the patient's ability to resume daily activities safely. Treatment may be adjusted according to progress.

  7. 07

    Long-Term Follow-up

    Ongoing care helps maintain seizure control, monitor changes in health, and identify any need for further treatment. Patients should continue follow-up as advised, even when seizures become less frequent or stop.

Benefits of Early Epilepsy Treatment

Early diagnosis and appropriate treatment can help reduce the impact of epilepsy on daily life. The benefits depend on the cause of the condition, the type of seizures, and the patient's response to treatment.

Potential benefits include:

  • Better seizure control
  • Reduced risk of seizure-related injuries
  • Improved confidence and independence
  • Better participation in school, work, and daily activities
  • Identification of medication side effects
  • Early recognition of drug-resistant epilepsy
  • Timely assessment for advanced treatment when appropriate
  • Improved long-term management and quality of life

Early evaluation also helps patients and families understand seizure first aid, medication adherence, and safety precautions.

Risks of Untreated or Poorly Controlled Epilepsy

Untreated epilepsy or recurrent seizures that remain poorly controlled can affect physical safety, emotional well-being, independence, and daily functioning.

Possible complications include:

  • Injuries caused by falls or uncontrolled movements
  • Memory or concentration difficulties
  • Anxiety, low mood, or social concerns
  • Disruption to education or employment
  • Sleep-related difficulties
  • Reduced independence and quality of life
  • Status epilepticus, a prolonged seizure or repeated seizures without recovery that requires emergency treatment
  • Sudden unexpected death in epilepsy (SUDEP), a rare but serious risk associated with epilepsy

The risk of complications varies between individuals. Appropriate treatment, medication adherence, safety planning, and regular medical follow-up can help reduce risks.

A seizure lasting five minutes or longer, repeated seizures without recovery, or breathing difficulties after a seizure require emergency medical attention.

Recovery and Living with Epilepsy

Recovery and long-term management depend on the type of epilepsy and the treatment required. Many patients manage epilepsy through medication and regular review without needing surgery. Patients who undergo epilepsy surgery or VNS implantation may require additional recovery care and follow-up.

To support long-term management, patients are encouraged to:

  • Take prescribed medication consistently.
  • Maintain a regular sleep routine.
  • Keep a record of seizure episodes and possible triggers.
  • Follow safety advice provided by the treating team.
  • Inform family members or caregivers about seizure first aid.
  • Attend scheduled follow-up appointments.
  • Discuss exercise, travel, driving, and work-related precautions with their doctor.

People with epilepsy can often continue their education, careers, family life, and other activities when seizures are appropriately managed. Individual safety recommendations depend on seizure control and the person's circumstances.

Patients recovering from epilepsy surgery or VNS implantation should follow the treating team's instructions regarding wound care, activity, medication, and return to normal routines.

Can epilepsy be cured?

Some patients achieve long-term seizure freedom with treatment, while others require ongoing medication or additional therapies. The outcome depends on the type of epilepsy, its cause, and the response to treatment.

Does every seizure mean a person has epilepsy?

No. Seizures may occur due to several medical conditions or temporary disturbances. A doctor reviews the episode, medical history, and investigation results to determine whether epilepsy is present.

When should I consult an epilepsy specialist?

You should seek medical evaluation after a first unexplained seizure, recurrent seizure episodes, uncertain seizure-like symptoms, or seizures that continue despite medication. A prolonged seizure or repeated seizures without recovery require emergency medical attention.

What tests are used to diagnose epilepsy?

Evaluation may include a medical history, neurological examination, EEG, Video EEG, MRI Brain, and selected blood tests or additional investigations. The tests recommended depend on the patient's symptoms and clinical findings.

Can epilepsy be treated without surgery?

Yes. Many patients achieve good seizure control with anti-seizure medication. Surgery is considered only for carefully selected patients after detailed assessment, particularly when seizures remain uncontrolled despite appropriate medication.

What is drug-resistant epilepsy?

Drug-resistant epilepsy refers to seizures that continue despite appropriate treatment trials with two suitable and tolerated anti-seizure medication schedules. These patients may benefit from specialist evaluation to explore additional treatment options.

When is epilepsy surgery recommended?

Epilepsy surgery may be considered when seizures remain uncontrolled despite appropriate medication and investigations identify a seizure focus that can be treated safely. The potential benefits and risks must be assessed for each patient.

What is Vagus Nerve Stimulation (VNS)?

VNS is a treatment that uses an implanted device to deliver electrical impulses to the vagus nerve. It may help reduce seizure frequency in selected patients, but it does not cure epilepsy and results vary.

Can children develop epilepsy?

Yes. Epilepsy can occur at any age. Treatment for children depends on the seizure type, cause, developmental needs, and response to medication. Some children may require additional investigations or advanced treatment.

Can people with epilepsy lead an active life?

Many people with epilepsy study, work, travel, and participate in daily activities when their condition is appropriately managed. Safety precautions and driving eligibility depend on individual circumstances and applicable regulations.

What is the cost of epilepsy treatment in Bangalore?

The cost depends on the consultation, diagnostic tests, medication, follow-up requirements, and whether advanced treatment is needed. An estimate can be discussed after the patient's condition and treatment requirements have been assessed.

Should I stop medication if I no longer experience seizures?

Do not stop anti-seizure medication without consulting your treating doctor. Any decision to reduce or discontinue medication requires an individual assessment because stopping it can increase the risk of recurrent seizures.
