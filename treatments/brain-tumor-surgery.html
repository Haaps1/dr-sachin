Overview

What is a brain tumour?

A brain tumour is an abnormal growth of cells in or around the brain. Primary tumours start in the brain itself, while secondary (metastatic) tumours spread from cancer elsewhere in the body. Tumours may be benign (slow-growing, non-cancerous) or malignant (faster-growing, cancerous).

Even a benign tumour can press on vital areas inside the closed space of the skull. Brain tumour surgery aims to remove as much of the tumour as is safely possible while protecting speech, movement and memory. Every operation is planned on advanced MRI so the route to the tumour avoids critical brain areas.

Benign tumours

Slow-growing with clear edges. Often cured by complete removal, e.g. most meningiomas.

Malignant tumours

Grow faster and invade nearby brain. Surgery is combined with radiotherapy or chemotherapy.

Types

Common types of brain tumours.

There are more than 100 kinds of brain tumour. These are the ones Dr Sachin treats most often.

01

Gliomas

The most common primary brain tumours, growing from the brain's support cells. They range from slow-growing (low grade) to fast-growing (high grade, such as glioblastoma).

02

Meningiomas

Usually benign tumours of the brain's coverings. Many can be cured with complete surgical removal.

03

Pituitary adenomas

Tumours of the hormone gland at the skull base that can affect vision and hormones. Most are removed through the nose.

04

Acoustic neuromas

Benign tumours (vestibular schwannomas) on the hearing and balance nerve, causing hearing loss, ringing or imbalance.

05

Brain metastases

Tumours that spread to the brain from cancers elsewhere, such as lung or breast. Surgery can relieve pressure and symptoms.

06

Paediatric tumours

Tumours such as medulloblastoma and craniopharyngioma that occur mainly in children and need child-focused care.

Symptoms

Signs of a brain tumour.

Symptoms depend on the tumour's size and where it sits in the brain. They often develop slowly.

01

Headaches that are new, worsening or worse in the morning

02

Seizures starting in adulthood

03

Weakness, numbness or trouble with speech or vision

04

A tumour found on a CT or MRI scan

Symptoms by location in the brain

Frontal lobe

Personality or behaviour change, weakness on one side, difficulty speaking or planning

Temporal lobe

Seizures, memory problems, difficulty understanding language, unusual smells

Parietal lobe

Numbness, trouble with reading, writing or judging distance

Occipital lobe

Loss of part of the vision on one side

Cerebellum

Unsteady walking, poor coordination, dizziness

Brainstem & skull base

Double vision, facial numbness, swallowing difficulty, hearing loss

Diagnosis

How a brain tumour is diagnosed.

Accurate diagnosis and careful mapping come before any decision about surgery.

01

MRI with contrast

The key scan. It shows the tumour's size, location and relationship to important brain areas.

02

Functional MRI & DTI

Maps speech and movement areas and the nerve fibre tracts, so surgery can avoid them.

03

MR spectroscopy & perfusion

Give clues about how active or aggressive a tumour may be.

04

CT scan

Useful in emergencies and for showing bone, calcium or bleeding.

05

Neurological examination

Tests of strength, sensation, vision, balance and memory to see how the tumour is affecting you.

06

Biopsy & molecular tests

Tissue confirms the exact tumour type and genetic markers that guide further treatment.

Treatment options

The right operation for your tumour.

Treatment is planned around the tumour's type, size and location, and your overall health and wishes.

Craniotomy

A small, precisely planned window in the skull to remove the tumour under the microscope, guided by neuronavigation.

Awake craniotomy

For tumours near speech or movement areas. You are comfortable and pain-free, and help the team protect these functions during surgery.

Endoscopic endonasal surgery

Pituitary and skull base tumours are removed through the nose with an endoscope, with no cut on the face or head.

Keyhole surgery

Selected tumours are reached through a small opening, for example in the eyebrow, for faster recovery.

Stereotactic biopsy

A needle guided to deep or multiple tumours through a tiny hole, to confirm the diagnosis safely.

Radiotherapy & chemotherapy

Planned with the oncology team after surgery when needed, or as the main treatment for some tumours.

Surgery day

What happens step by step.

Every step is planned around your scans, your symptoms and your goals.

STEP 01

Planning

Your MRI is loaded into the navigation system and the safest route is planned.

STEP 02

Anaesthesia

General anaesthesia, or scalp block and sedation for awake surgery.

STEP 03

Guided opening

A small, targeted opening placed exactly over the tumour.

STEP 04

Microsurgical removal

Tumour removed under the microscope with nerve monitoring.

STEP 05

Neuro-ICU care

Close monitoring overnight, then back to the ward.

Recovery

Your recovery timeline.

Every recovery is different. This is what most patients can expect.

Day 1

Monitored in the neuro-ICU. Most patients sit up, talk and eat the same or next day.

Days 2–5

Walking in the ward, a check scan, and discharge home once stable.

Weeks 2–6

Stitches removed, energy returns, and light work often resumes. Driving only when cleared.

Month 3 onward

Follow-up MRI and, if needed, radiotherapy or chemotherapy with regular reviews.

Risks, and how they are reduced

Every operation carries some risk. Careful planning, navigation, monitoring and an experienced team keep these as low as possible. Possible risks include:

  • Bleeding or swelling of the brain
  • Infection of the wound
  • Seizures
  • Temporary or permanent weakness, numbness or speech difficulty
  • Leak of brain fluid (CSF)
  • Blood clots in the legs

Preparing for surgery

  • Bring all scans, reports and a list of your medicines
  • Tell us about blood thinners; some need to be stopped
  • Blood tests, ECG and an anaesthesia check are done before surgery
  • Nothing to eat or drink for 6–8 hours before the operation
  • Plan for a family member to stay with you after discharge

After discharge, call straight away for fever, a severe headache, new weakness, a seizure, or fluid leaking from the wound.

FAQs

Brain tumour questions.

Every case is different. Your own questions are answered in detail at your consultation.

Ask on WhatsApp
Is every brain tumour cancer?

No. Many brain tumours, such as most meningiomas, pituitary adenomas and acoustic neuromas, are benign. Benign tumours can still cause problems because of where they are, so they may still need treatment.

Is brain tumour surgery safe?

With neuronavigation, the operating microscope, awake mapping and nerve monitoring, brain surgery is far safer than before. Your individual risks depend on the tumour's size and location and are explained in detail before any decision.

Will my whole head be shaved?

No. Usually only a narrow strip of hair along the incision is trimmed, and the scar is hidden once the hair grows back.

Is awake brain surgery painful?

No. The scalp is numbed and the brain itself does not feel pain. You are given sedation and kept comfortable, and are only fully awake for the mapping part.

How long will I stay in hospital?

Most patients go home in 3–7 days, depending on the tumour and the operation.

Will the tumour come back?

Many benign tumours are cured by complete removal. Some tumours can recur, which is why regular follow-up MRI scans are part of your care.

Will I need radiation or chemotherapy?

It depends on the final tissue diagnosis. The biopsy and molecular results guide whether further treatment is needed.

When can I return to work and drive?

Desk work is often possible in 4–6 weeks. Driving depends on the tumour and whether you have had seizures, and is only allowed once you are cleared.
