What is a Meningioma?

A meningioma is a tumour that develops from the meninges, the protective membranes that surround the brain and spinal cord. Unlike many other brain tumours that arise from brain tissue itself, meningiomas originate from these covering layers. As the tumour grows, it may press against the brain, spinal cord, blood vessels, or cranial nerves, leading to a variety of neurological symptoms.

Most meningiomas are benign (non-cancerous) and grow slowly over many years. However, some may behave more aggressively or recur after treatment. Even a benign meningioma can become clinically significant if it compresses important areas of the brain.

The treatment approach depends on several factors, including the tumour's size, location, WHO grade, growth pattern, associated symptoms, and the patient's overall health. An experienced neurosurgeon plays a key role in determining whether observation, surgery, or additional therapies are the most appropriate option.

Types of Meningioma

Meningiomas are classified according to their biological behaviour and their location within the brain.

WHO Grade 1 Meningioma

Grade 1 meningiomas are the most common type and are generally slow-growing. Many can be successfully managed with surgery, while small asymptomatic tumours may only require regular monitoring.

WHO Grade 2 (Atypical) Meningioma

These tumours grow faster than Grade 1 meningiomas and have a higher chance of recurrence. Treatment often involves surgery followed by close follow-up or additional therapy in selected cases.

WHO Grade 3 (Anaplastic) Meningioma

Grade 3 meningiomas are rare and more aggressive. They usually require comprehensive treatment, including surgery and adjuvant therapies such as radiation.

Common Locations of Meningiomas

The location of a meningioma significantly influences both symptoms and treatment planning.

Some common locations include:

  • Convexity meningioma
  • Parasagittal meningioma
  • Falcine meningioma
  • Skull base meningioma
  • Sphenoid wing meningioma
  • Olfactory groove meningioma
  • Tuberculum sellae meningioma
  • Cerebellopontine angle (CPA) meningioma
  • Spinal meningioma

Tumours located at the skull base or near major blood vessels and cranial nerves often require highly specialized surgical expertise due to their complexity.

Causes and Risk Factors

The exact cause of most meningiomas remains unknown. However, several factors are associated with an increased risk of developing these tumours.

These include:

  • Increasing age
  • Female gender
  • Previous exposure to radiation therapy involving the head
  • Certain inherited genetic disorders such as Neurofibromatosis Type 2 (NF2)
  • Hormonal influences
  • Family history in rare cases

Although these factors may increase risk, many patients diagnosed with meningioma have no identifiable cause.

Symptoms of Meningioma

Many meningiomas remain asymptomatic for years and are discovered incidentally during brain imaging performed for unrelated reasons. As the tumour enlarges, it may compress nearby brain tissue or nerves, leading to neurological symptoms.

Common symptoms include:

  • Persistent headaches
  • Seizures
  • Weakness or numbness in the arms or legs
  • Vision changes
  • Hearing loss
  • Difficulty with balance or coordination
  • Memory problems
  • Personality or behavioural changes
  • Speech difficulties
  • Loss of smell
  • Facial numbness or weakness

Symptoms vary depending on the tumour's location. For example, skull base meningiomas may affect vision, facial sensation, hearing, or swallowing, while convexity meningiomas more commonly present with seizures or weakness.

When Should You Consult a Neurosurgeon?

Not every headache or neurological symptom is caused by a brain tumour. However, persistent or progressive symptoms should never be ignored.

You should consult a neurosurgeon if you experience:

  • Persistent headaches that continue to worsen
  • New-onset seizures
  • Weakness in the arms or legs
  • Vision or hearing changes
  • Difficulty speaking
  • Memory loss or confusion
  • Balance problems
  • Facial numbness
  • Symptoms following the diagnosis of a brain tumour on MRI

Early consultation helps establish an accurate diagnosis and allows timely treatment before permanent neurological damage occurs.

Diagnosis of Meningioma

Accurate diagnosis is essential for planning the most appropriate meningioma treatment.

The evaluation begins with a detailed neurological examination followed by advanced imaging studies.

01

MRI Brain

MRI is the preferred investigation for diagnosing meningiomas. It provides detailed information about the tumour's size, location, blood supply, and relationship to surrounding brain tissue.

02

CT Scan

A CT scan helps evaluate bone involvement and calcification, which are commonly seen in certain meningiomas.

Advanced Imaging

Depending on the complexity of the tumour, additional investigations such as MR Angiography, MR Venography, Functional MRI, or diffusion imaging may be performed to assist surgical planning.

Histopathological Examination

Following meningioma treatment surgery, the tumour tissue is examined under the microscope to determine its WHO grade and biological behaviour. This information helps guide further treatment and long-term follow-up.

Personalized Treatment Planning

Every patient is evaluated individually before deciding on the best treatment strategy.

Factors considered include:

  • Tumour size
  • Tumour location
  • WHO grade
  • Growth rate
  • Neurological symptoms
  • Patient's age
  • Overall health
  • Risk of recurrence

After reviewing all findings, Dr. Sachin develops a personalized treatment plan focused on achieving the best possible clinical outcome while preserving neurological function.

Treatment Options for Meningioma

The treatment for a meningioma depends on several factors, including the tumour's size, location, WHO grade, growth rate, symptoms, age, and overall health of the patient. While some meningiomas can be safely monitored, others require surgery or additional therapies to prevent neurological complications and improve long-term outcomes.

Dr. Sachin G.R. believes that every patient deserves an individualized treatment plan rather than a one-size-fits-all approach. After a detailed evaluation and review of imaging studies, the most appropriate treatment strategy is recommended based on the patient's condition and current international clinical guidelines.

Observation and Active Surveillance

Not every meningioma requires immediate treatment. Small, slow-growing tumours that are not causing symptoms may only need regular monitoring with periodic MRI scans and neurological examinations.

Observation may be recommended when:

  • The tumour is small and discovered incidentally.
  • There are no significant neurological symptoms.
  • Imaging shows no evidence of tumour growth.
  • The potential risks of surgery outweigh the benefits.

Regular follow-up helps detect any changes in tumour size or behaviour so that treatment can be initiated if required.

When is Meningioma Surgery Recommended?

Surgery remains the primary treatment for many symptomatic or growing meningiomas. The objective is to remove the tumour safely while preserving surrounding brain tissue, cranial nerves, and major blood vessels.

Meningioma surgery may be recommended when:

  • The tumour is increasing in size.
  • Persistent headaches, seizures, or neurological symptoms are present.
  • There is pressure on the brain or spinal cord.
  • Vision, hearing, speech, or balance is affected.
  • Imaging suggests a higher-grade or atypical meningioma.
  • Tissue diagnosis is required to determine the tumour type.

For many patients, meningioma surgical treatment offers the best opportunity for long-term tumour control and symptom relief.

Meningioma Surgery

The aim of meningioma treatment surgery is to remove as much of the tumour as safely possible while preserving neurological function. The extent of tumour removal depends on the tumour's location and its relationship with critical brain structures.

Whenever possible, complete removal of both the tumour and its point of attachment is performed to reduce the likelihood of recurrence. In complex cases where the tumour is closely associated with important nerves or blood vessels, a small portion may be intentionally left behind to avoid neurological injury. If required, additional treatment such as radiation therapy may be recommended for the residual tumour.

The goals of meningioma brain treatment include:

  • Relieving pressure on the brain
  • Improving neurological symptoms
  • Preventing further tumour growth
  • Preserving neurological function
  • Reducing the risk of recurrence
  • Providing tissue for definitive diagnosis

Advanced Surgical Techniques

01

Microsurgical Meningioma Removal

Modern meningioma surgery in Bangalore is performed using microsurgical techniques that provide excellent visualization of delicate brain structures. High-powered operating microscopes and specialized instruments allow precise tumour removal while protecting healthy tissue.

02

Skull Base Meningioma Surgery

Tumours located at the skull base are among the most technically challenging brain tumours because they lie close to the cranial nerves, brainstem, and major blood vessels.

Dr. Sachin's Clinical Fellowship in Skull Base Surgery from Osaka, Japan, provides advanced expertise in managing these complex tumours. Careful preoperative planning and meticulous microsurgical techniques help maximize tumour removal while minimizing neurological complications.

03

Image-Guided Brain Surgery

Neuronavigation acts as a GPS system during surgery, allowing accurate localization of the tumour using preoperative MRI and CT images. This improves surgical precision and helps protect critical brain structures.

04

Awake Brain Surgery

Although less commonly required for meningiomas than gliomas, Awake Brain Surgery may be considered for selected tumours located near areas controlling speech or movement. Continuous neurological assessment during surgery helps preserve essential brain functions.

Advanced Technologies Used During Surgery

Successful treatment for meningioma brain tumour relies not only on surgical expertise but also on advanced technology that enhances precision and safety.

Neuronavigation

Provides real-time guidance during surgery, helping accurately locate the tumour and define safe surgical boundaries.

Operating Microscope

Offers high magnification and illumination, enabling delicate microsurgical dissection around nerves and blood vessels.

Intraoperative Neuromonitoring

Monitors neurological pathways throughout surgery, allowing early detection of changes in nerve function and improving patient safety.

Brain Mapping

Used in selected patients when tumours are located close to areas responsible for speech, movement, or sensation.

Together, these technologies support safer surgery, improved tumour removal, and better functional outcomes.

Radiation Therapy for Meningioma

Radiation therapy may be recommended in selected situations, particularly when complete surgical removal is not possible or when the tumour shows a higher risk of recurrence.

It may be considered for:

  • Residual tumour after surgery
  • Recurrent meningioma
  • WHO Grade 2 or Grade 3 meningiomas
  • Patients who are not suitable candidates for surgery

The decision is based on tumour characteristics, pathology findings, and the overall treatment plan.

Can Meningioma Be Treated Without Surgery?

Yes. Not every patient requires surgery.

Treatment without surgery may be appropriate when:

  • The tumour is very small.
  • There are no symptoms.
  • MRI shows no significant growth.
  • The patient has medical conditions that increase surgical risk.

In these situations, regular MRI scans and neurological follow-up help monitor the tumour over time.

Benefits of Meningioma Surgery

For appropriately selected patients, meningioma surgery offers several important benefits:

  • Relief from headaches and seizures
  • Improvement in neurological symptoms
  • Reduction of pressure on the brain
  • Better quality of life
  • Prevention of further tumour growth
  • Accurate pathological diagnosis
  • Reduced risk of recurrence when complete removal is achieved

Risks and Possible Complications

Like all major neurosurgical procedures, meningioma treatment surgery carries certain risks. These vary depending on the tumour's size, location, and complexity.

Possible complications include:

  • Bleeding
  • Infection
  • Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) leak
  • Temporary or permanent neurological deficits
  • Stroke
  • Seizures
  • Brain swelling
  • Anaesthesia-related complications

Careful surgical planning, advanced imaging, neuronavigation, and intraoperative monitoring help reduce these risks and improve patient safety.

Recovery After Meningioma Surgery

Recovery depends on the tumour's location, the extent of surgery, and the patient's overall neurological condition.

After surgery, patients are closely monitored in the intensive care unit before being transferred to the regular ward. Most patients gradually begin walking, eating, and performing routine activities within a few days under medical supervision.

Recovery generally includes:

  • Neurological monitoring
  • Pain management
  • Wound care
  • Repeat imaging when required
  • Gradual mobilization
  • Follow-up consultations

The duration of recovery varies from person to person, and additional rehabilitation may be recommended depending on neurological function.

Rehabilitation After Treatment

Some patients may require rehabilitation to regain strength, coordination, balance, speech, or cognitive function after meningioma treatment.

Depending on individual needs, rehabilitation may include:

  • Physiotherapy
  • Occupational therapy
  • Speech and language therapy
  • Cognitive rehabilitation
  • Psychological counselling
  • Nutritional guidance

A structured rehabilitation programme supports recovery and helps patients return to their daily activities with greater confidence.

Factors that influence the cost include:

Why Early Diagnosis Matters

Many meningiomas grow slowly and may not cause symptoms for years. However, as the tumour enlarges, it can compress nearby brain tissue, cranial nerves, or blood vessels, leading to neurological complications.

Early diagnosis offers several important advantages:

  • More treatment options
  • Better surgical planning

What is a meningioma?

A meningioma is a tumour that develops from the meninges, the protective membranes surrounding the brain and spinal cord. Most meningiomas are benign, but some may grow more aggressively and require specialized treatment.

Are all meningiomas cancerous?

No. The majority of meningiomas are benign (WHO Grade 1). Some are atypical (Grade 2) or malignant (Grade 3), which may require more extensive treatment and closer follow-up.

Does every meningioma require surgery?

No. Small, slow-growing tumours without symptoms may only require regular MRI scans and neurological follow-up. Surgery is recommended when the tumour is growing, causing symptoms, or compressing important brain structures.

What is the best treatment for meningioma?

The best treatment depends on the tumour's size, location, WHO grade, symptoms, and overall health of the patient. Management may include observation, surgery, radiation therapy, or a combination of treatments.

Is meningioma surgery safe?

Modern neurosurgical techniques, neuronavigation, microsurgery, and intraoperative neuromonitoring have significantly improved the safety of meningioma surgery. The overall risk depends on the tumour's location and complexity.

Can a meningioma come back after surgery?

Some meningiomas can recur, particularly higher-grade tumours or those that cannot be completely removed. Regular follow-up MRI scans help detect recurrence at an early stage.

How long does recovery take after meningioma surgery?

Recovery varies depending on the complexity of the surgery and the patient's neurological condition. Many patients gradually return to normal activities over several weeks, while others may require rehabilitation.

Will I need radiation therapy after surgery?

Radiation therapy may be recommended if part of the tumour remains after surgery, if the tumour recurs, or if pathology shows a higher-grade meningioma.

What factors affect meningioma treatment cost?

The cost depends on the tumour's location, complexity, investigations required, surgical approach, hospital stay, intensive care needs, and whether additional treatments such as radiation therapy are necessary.

When should I seek a second opinion?

A second opinion is advisable if you have been diagnosed with a meningioma, advised to undergo surgery, or wish to understand all available treatment options before making a decision.

Can patients live a normal life after meningioma treatment?
